Implementation

Choose a realistic, ethical effective date.

Your guide recommends providing clients at least 30 days' notice and ideally 45–60 days. It also suggests considering a business anniversary, a credential milestone, or a new quarter as a natural review point.

Choose both dates to check your proposed notice period.

Before implementation, review the actual fee currently disclosed in your practice paperwork, any existing client agreements, and your policies for transitions or exceptions. Update the website, intake documents and financial policies consistently, and document applicable notices.

Know which rate you can actually change

The guide discusses setting your own session fee. An insurance-contracted allowed amount generally cannot be increased unilaterally by simply changing your billed charge. Review each payer contract, network rules, fee schedule and applicable notice requirements. Client responsibility (including deductibles, coinsurance and copays) is also governed by plan and contract terms. For insurance sessions, use a negotiated increase or a documented payer-mix scenario, not the private-pay client notice template by default.
Payer strategy · Insurance reimbursement

What if you want a 5%–15% increase from insurance?

When you're in network, the amount you receive is usually determined by the contract's allowed amount or fee schedule, not simply the amount you enter as your billed charge. Requesting an increase is different from receiving an approved increase. Use this activity to prepare for a payer conversation.

Two different rate changes

Private-pay fee: You set a fee subject to applicable obligations, client agreements, and notice requirements.

Insurance-contracted reimbursement: Review your contract and amendment terms, request a rate review or negotiation from the payer, and only treat a new amount as effective when confirmed under the contract.

Do not automatically increase an insured client's deductible, copay, or coinsurance to make up the difference. Do not treat a new billed charge as a guaranteed higher payment. Government programs, managed care and platform contracts can have different rules.

Research local benchmarks with FAIR Health Consumer

Before drafting your insurance rate-review request, start by checking FAIR Health Consumer — Medical Cost Lookup to research published cost estimates for your area. FAIR Health uses claims data to show separate provider billed charges and estimated in-network allowed amounts, with percentile ranges. For a payer negotiation, the estimated in-network allowed amount is the more relevant comparison to a contracted allowed amount—but it is not that insurer's actual fee schedule or a guaranteed negotiated rate.

Try this research activity
  1. Open FAIR Health Consumer and select Medical Costs.
  2. Look up the same relevant CPT code and local geographic area as your rate request. If no estimate is available for that code or location, record N/A rather than guessing.
  3. Compare available in-network allowed amount estimates and percentiles with your actual contracted allowed amount and paid claims. Record the percentile, source and date; do not substitute billed-charge estimates for reimbursement benchmarks.
  4. Use the findings as supporting context alongside payer-specific contracts, paid remittances, network-access evidence and service volume—not as proof the payer owes a particular rate.

If FAIR Health does not display a relevant estimate: write “N/A” in your notes, then use your actual paid claims, current agreement, documented referral/network-access needs, and other legitimately obtained comparison data. A missing estimate should not block the request.

Communication practice

How will you explain the increase?

Use the guide's client notification framework: appreciation, a clear effective date and new rate, plus an invitation to discuss appropriate options.

Suggested response

What could you actually say? Practice with these examples.

Read the sample response aloud, then rewrite it in your own voice above. These are conversation starting points, not promises of an accommodation or change to a clinically indicated treatment plan.

When announcing a private-pay fee change

“I want to give you advance notice that my private-pay session fee will change from $120 to $132 beginning [date]. I value our work together, and I’m happy to answer questions so you have time to plan.”

When someone says they cannot afford it

“Thank you for telling me. I understand that a fee change affects your budget. We can discuss what options may be appropriate for your care, including referrals if needed.”

When someone asks why

“I periodically review my fees based on the costs of running the practice, my experience, and the sustainability of the services I provide. I want you to have clear notice of the change.”

When a client asks whether insurance will cover the new fee

“If we are in network, your plan’s contracted terms and benefits determine what applies. I’ll have our billing process verify the applicable fee and your estimated patient responsibility rather than assume the private-pay increase applies to your insurance.”

These example amounts come from your guide’s $120 → $132 illustration. Replace them with the member’s actual approved fee and effective date. For insured patients, follow the network agreement and program rules.
